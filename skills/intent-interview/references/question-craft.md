# Question craft

How to find the right questions for this project, rather than reusing the same ones every time. The interview gives you principles, a repertoire of moves, and a way to generate candidates; the questions themselves get written fresh for each project.

The examples below come from deliberately varied projects (a mastering studio, a tool library, a bakery, a climbing gym, a museum exhibit, a budgeting app). They show the move, not the wording. An example placed in front of a model becomes a template, so never reuse an example's sentence: write each question in the project's own nouns and the user's own words.

## What makes a question good

Use these as the filter before you ask anything.

1. **The user can answer it from what they know:** their project, their people, their experience, their convictions. A question about colours, fonts, layouts, identity or visual taste fails here; they haven't seen anything yet, and the translation is your job.
2. **It belongs to this project.** It's built from the project's nouns, people and the user's own words. If the same question could be asked of any project, it will get a generic answer; sharpen it until it couldn't.
3. **It earns its place.** Its answer could change the direction, or it could open a line of thought you don't have yet. A question that only confirms what you already know is spending the user's attention for nothing.
4. **It asks for something concrete before something abstract,** and asks one main thing.
5. **It's clean.** It uses the user's words and doesn't plant your metaphor or your answer, unless you're deliberately testing a reading.
6. **It's a different move from the question before it.** Seven questions using the same move get seven versions of one answer.

## How to generate them

Before each round, question-storm privately. Write 15 to 25 candidate questions drawn from at least four of the families below, in the project's own terms, without judging them as you go. Separating generating from choosing produces better questions than writing a polished few at once. Then choose 3 to 7 that pass the filter, cover at least three families, and don't repeat a move. In round two, favour candidates aimed at where the strongest seeds disagree, and keep one that goes somewhere none of them reach.

## The moves, by what they reveal

### Moments: what actually happens

General questions get the person people want to be. Specific episodes get what actually happens, with detail a model can design from.

- **The last real time.** Ask about the most recent actual instance of the problem, the visit or the use. (Critical incident technique.) *Climbing gym:* "Tell me about the last person who came in for their first session. What happened in their first ten minutes?"
- **Reaching for it.** Reconstruct the moment someone turns to the project: what pushed them, what pulled them, what nearly stopped them. (Jobs to be done switch interviews.) *Budgeting app:* "What was going on in someone's week when they finally decided they needed this?"
- **Best and worst.** Ask for the single best moment and the single worst, not the typical one. Extremes carry the most information. (Critical incidents; appreciative inquiry.) *Bakery:* "What's the best morning you've had behind the counter? And the one you'd rather forget?"
- **The scene.** Where are people, when, what's in their hands, what can they hear? This is the richest source of atmosphere. *Museum exhibit:* "Where has a visitor just come from when they walk into this room, and what's the light like?"
- **The extremes of the audience.** Ask about the most devoted person, the most unusual one, and the one who left. Designs that work for the edges often work better for the middle. (IDEO, extremes and mainstreams.) *Tool library:* "Who's your most enthusiastic borrower, and who signed up once and never came back?"

### Motives: why it matters

- **Ladder up.** Ask why something matters, then why that matters, until you reach a value. Use it on goals and experiences, never on visual likes. (Laddering.) *Mastering studio:* "You said clients should feel looked after. What changes for them when they feel that?"
- **Pyramid down.** The opposite move: when an answer is abstract, ask for an example, or how someone would know. "Premium", "honest" and "warm" become usable once they're grounded. (Pyramiding.) *Bakery:* "You said the shop should feel honest. What's something a customer could notice that would tell them that?"
- **The three jobs.** Every project does a practical job, an emotional job, and a social job, meaning what using it says about someone. The social job is the one most briefs miss. (Jobs to be done.) *Climbing gym:* "What does it say about someone that this is their gym?"
- **Origin.** What was the user reacting against when they started? Origins hold convictions. *Budgeting app:* "What made you angry enough about money apps to build your own?"
- **Absence.** If the project disappeared tomorrow, what would people miss that they couldn't get elsewhere? This finds the core value without asking for a mission statement. *Tool library:* "If the library shut next month, what would the street lose that a hardware store couldn't replace?"

### Difference: where it stands

- **Where the field goes wrong.** Ask what others in the field get wrong, or what people believe about it that isn't true. *Museum exhibit:* "What do most exhibitions on this subject get wrong?"
- **Onlyness.** Ask the user to finish a sentence: "we're the only ___ that ___". If they can't, that's useful too. (Neumeier, Zag.) *Mastering studio:* "Finish this: we're the only studio that ___."
- **Never to be mistaken for.** Which place, brand or project in their field would they hate to be confused with, and why? It's a question about positioning, which they know, not about style. *Bakery:* "Which bakery would you be gutted to be compared to?"
- **The wave.** What's changing in the field or the culture that this project rides on, or pushes against? *Budgeting app:* "What's shifted in how people your age think about money in the last few years?"

### Tension: where it gets specific

- **The paradox.** Name the tension you've noticed and ask how the user lives with it. *Mastering studio:* "Clients want to trust you completely and also stay in charge of their music. How do you handle that in a real session?"
- **Boundaries.** Adjacent qualities blur: precise turns clinical, playful turns childish, premium turns pretentious. Ask where the line is. *Climbing gym:* "You want it to feel serious about climbing. When does serious start to feel unwelcoming?"
- **Triads in words.** Describe three scenes, people or situations from the project and ask which two belong together and what makes the third different. The answer comes out as the user's own contrast, in their own words. (Repertory grid.) *Tool library:* "Three borrowers: someone fixing a fence before rain, someone building a cubby for their kid, someone who just wants to try a tool. Which two is this really for?"
- **What-if provocations.** Thought experiments about the project, not the design, that force a priority to show. *Museum exhibit:* "If visitors could only take away one thing from the room, what should it be?" *Bakery:* "If you could only sell three things, which three?"

### Time: what it's for

- **Distance.** Ask about the project years out. Thinking further ahead makes people think more abstractly, which helps creative insight. (Construal level research.) *Budgeting app:* "If this is still around in ten years, what will people say it changed?"
- **Pre-mortem.** "It's a year after launch and it didn't work. What went wrong?" Imagining the outcome has already happened produces about 30 percent more reasons than asking what might happen. (Prospective hindsight; Klein's pre-mortem.) *Climbing gym:* "A year from now, new members aren't sticking around. Why?"
- **Working backwards.** What would the one-paragraph launch announcement say? Or what would a visitor tell a friend afterwards? (Amazon's working backwards.) *Tool library:* "What do you hope someone says to a neighbour after their first borrow?"
- **Peak and end.** People remember an experience by its strongest moment and how it ended. Ask which moment should be remembered and how people should leave. It's about the experience, which the user can answer, not the screen. (Kahneman's peak-end rule; the Heaths' elevation, insight, pride and connection.) *Museum exhibit:* "What's the moment you'd want people talking about on the train home?"

### Their language: going deeper on what they've said

- **Clean follow-ups.** Use the user's exact words and add as little of your own as possible. "What kind of [their word] is that [their word]?" "Is there anything else about [their word]?" "That [their word] is like what?" These open up a metaphor or a vague word without steering it. (Clean Language.)
- **Push their metaphor.** When the user offers a metaphor, explore it. Cold metaphor questions ("if your brand were an animal…") feel like homework; their own metaphors don't. *Mastering studio:* "You called it a listening room. What happens in a listening room that doesn't happen in a studio?"
- **Name their tension back.** Reflect a tension they stated in passing and ask which side wins when the two collide.

### Indirect: when answers come out generic

Use these sparingly, when direct questions keep returning clichés or the user gets stuck. They work because they lower the pressure to sound sensible, and they can feel like games if overused. (Projective techniques; Smart Design's love and breakup letters.)

- **Finish the sentence.** "Working with us feels like ___." "People leave the shop feeling ___."
- **The honest letter, in one line.** "What would the most honest thank-you note from a client say?" or "If someone stopped coming, what would their reason really be?"
- **Show me something.** Invite, never require, a photo of an object, a place or a moment from the project's world. A single image from their world beats a paragraph about it.

## Round by round

- **Round one** leans on Moments, Motives (especially origin and absence), Difference, and the scene. Concrete first.
- **Round two** leans on Tension, Their language, Motives (ladder and pyramid), and Time (pre-mortem, peak and end). Bring in Indirect when answers are thin.
- **Round three** is about the reading boards: what each gets right and wrong for the project, what they'd keep from each, and what's missing from all three. Ask for parts and reactions, never reasons for a visual like and never "which one?".

## Questions to avoid

- Questions that hand the design back: colours, fonts, styles, moods, "what would make it feel unmistakably yours?".
- Asking why they like a visual. Explaining a preference makes people's judgments worse; reactions are reliable, reasons aren't.
- Brand-questionnaire staples: "describe your brand in three to five words", "who is your target audience?", "what's your unique selling point?". The user can answer them, but they produce aspirations and clichés. Ask the episode version instead.
- Multiple choice, which turns discovery into a vote on options they haven't seen.
- Stacked questions that hide two or three asks in one.
